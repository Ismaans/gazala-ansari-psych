Know Your Provider

An educational guide to mental health credentials — so you can understand who does what, and choose the care that is right for you.

Understanding Mental Health Credentials

Psychologist (PhD / PsyD)

A psychologist holds a doctoral degree — either a PhD (Doctor of Philosophy) or PsyD (Doctor of Psychology) — requiring 5–7 years of graduate training, a supervised internship of approximately 2,000 clinical hours, and post-doctoral residency. Psychologists are extensively trained in psychotherapy, psychological assessment, and neuropsychological evaluation. They can diagnose mental health conditions and conduct formal testing for ADHD, learning disabilities, and cognitive function.

In Virginia, psychologists cannot prescribe medication. This is an important distinction: psychologists are specialists in the mind and behavior — not medication management. If medication is needed, they coordinate with a psychiatrist or primary care physician.

Psychiatrist (MD / DO)

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who completed medical school followed by a psychiatric residency. Because of their medical training, psychiatrists are qualified to prescribe and manage psychiatric medications. However, the majority of psychiatrists today focus primarily on medication management — typically seeing patients for brief 15-minute "medication check" appointments — rather than providing ongoing talk therapy. Research published in the European Journal of Psychiatry (2024) found that most community mental health psychiatrists see patients for 15-minute medication visits every 1–3 months, which is fundamentally different from the depth of ongoing psychotherapy. If you are seeking a therapist to process emotions, work through trauma, or develop coping strategies, a psychologist, social worker, or counselor is typically better suited to that work.

Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW / LICSW)

A Licensed Clinical Social Worker holds a Master of Social Work (MSW) degree and has completed thousands of hours of supervised clinical training. Social workers are trained not only in psychotherapy, but also in understanding how social systems, family dynamics, community resources, financial stress, and environmental factors affect mental health. LCSWs can independently diagnose and treat mental health conditions. They cannot prescribe medication.

Licensed Master Social Worker (LMSW)

An LMSW holds the same MSW degree as an LCSW but is in the process of accumulating the supervised clinical hours required for full independent licensure. An LMSW practices under the supervision of a licensed clinician and is actively building toward their LCSW.

Licensed Professional Counselor (LPC)

A Licensed Professional Counselor holds a master's degree in counseling or clinical mental health counseling and has completed thousands of supervised clinical hours (Virginia requires 3,400 hours). LPCs are trained in evidence-based therapy approaches such as CBT and can diagnose and treat mental health disorders. In Virginia, LPCs have full authority to diagnose mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders. They cannot prescribe medication.

Key Differences at a Glance

Provider Degree Prescribes Medication? Specializes In
Psychologist PhD / PsyD No (not in Virginia) Therapy, Testing & Evaluation
Psychiatrist MD / DO Yes Medication Management
LCSW / LICSW Master's (MSW) No Therapy + Social Systems
LMSW Master's (MSW) No Supervised Therapy
LPC Master's (Counseling) No Therapy & Counseling

LPC vs. LCSW — What's the Real Difference?

Both LPCs and LCSWs are master's-level clinicians who provide therapy and can independently diagnose mental health conditions. Their training, however, reflects different philosophies:

LPCs are trained primarily in counseling theory, clinical mental health techniques, human development, and evidence-based therapeutic interventions. Their curriculum centers on one-on-one and group therapy skills.

LCSWs are trained with a "person-in-environment" lens — meaning they understand not just the individual's psychology, but how their family, community, culture, financial situation, and systemic factors shape their mental health. Social workers are often more equipped to connect clients with community resources and navigate systems like hospitals, schools, and housing alongside therapy.

In practice, day-to-day therapy sessions with an LPC and an LCSW may feel quite similar. The difference shows in how each clinician conceptualizes your situation and what additional tools or resources they may bring to your care.
