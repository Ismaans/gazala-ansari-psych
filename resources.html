Know Your Provider

An educational guide to mental health credentials — so you can understand who does what, and choose the care that is right for you.

Understanding Mental Health Credentials

Psychologist (PhD / PsyD)

A psychologist holds a doctoral degree — either a PhD (Doctor of Philosophy) or PsyD (Doctor of Psychology) — requiring 5–7 years of graduate training, a supervised internship of approximately 2,000 clinical hours, and post-doctoral residency. Psychologists are extensively trained in psychotherapy, psychological assessment, and neuropsychological evaluation. They can diagnose mental health conditions and conduct formal testing for ADHD, learning disabilities, and cognitive function.

In Virginia, psychologists cannot prescribe medication. This is an important distinction: psychologists are specialists in the mind and behavior — not medication management. If medication is needed, they coordinate with a psychiatrist or primary care physician.

Psychiatrist (MD / DO)

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who completed medical school followed by a psychiatric residency. Because of their medical training, psychiatrists are qualified to prescribe and manage psychiatric medications. However, the majority of psychiatrists today focus primarily on medication management — typically seeing patients for brief 15-minute "medication check" appointments — rather than providing ongoing talk therapy. Research published in the European Journal of Psychiatry (2024) found that most community mental health psychiatrists see patients for 15-minute medication visits every 1–3 months, which is fundamentally different from the depth of ongoing psychotherapy. If you are seeking a therapist to process emotions, work through trauma, or develop coping strategies, a psychologist, social worker, or counselor is typically better suited to that work.

Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW / LICSW)

A Licensed Clinical Social Worker holds a Master of Social Work (MSW) degree and has completed thousands of hours of supervised clinical training. Social workers are trained not only in psychotherapy, but also in understanding how social systems, family dynamics, community resources, financial stress, and environmental factors affect mental health. LCSWs can independently diagnose and treat mental health conditions. They cannot prescribe medication.

Licensed Master Social Worker (LMSW)

An LMSW holds the same MSW degree as an LCSW but is in the process of accumulating the supervised clinical hours required for full independent licensure. An LMSW practices under the supervision of a licensed clinician and is actively building toward their LCSW.

Licensed Professional Counselor (LPC)

A Licensed Professional Counselor holds a master's degree in counseling or clinical mental health counseling and has completed thousands of supervised clinical hours (Virginia requires 3,400 hours). LPCs are trained in evidence-based therapy approaches such as CBT and can diagnose and treat mental health disorders. In Virginia, LPCs have full authority to diagnose mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders. They cannot prescribe medication.

Key Differences at a Glance

Provider Degree Prescribes Medication? Specializes In
Psychologist PhD / PsyD No (not in Virginia) Therapy, Testing & Evaluation
Psychiatrist MD / DO Yes Medication Management
LCSW / LICSW Master's (MSW) No Therapy + Social Systems
LMSW Master's (MSW) No Supervised Therapy
LPC Master's (Counseling) No Therapy & Counseling

LPC vs. LCSW — What's the Real Difference?

Both LPCs and LCSWs are master's-level clinicians who provide therapy and can independently diagnose mental health conditions. Their training, however, reflects different philosophies:

LPCs are trained primarily in counseling theory, clinical mental health techniques, human development, and evidence-based therapeutic interventions. Their curriculum centers on one-on-one and group therapy skills.

LCSWs are trained with a "person-in-environment" lens — meaning they understand not just the individual's psychology, but how their family, community, culture, financial situation, and systemic factors shape their mental health. Social workers are often more equipped to connect clients with community resources and navigate systems like hospitals, schools, and housing alongside therapy.

In practice, day-to-day therapy sessions with an LPC and an LCSW may feel quite similar. The difference shows in how each clinician conceptualizes your situation and what additional tools or resources they may bring to your care.

Why Local Matters

How a local, licensed, community-based practice compares to large online therapy platforms — and what to look for in any provider.

The Rise of Online Therapy Platforms

Online therapy platforms like BetterHelp, Talkspace, and similar services have marketed themselves as convenient, affordable alternatives to traditional therapy. For millions of people, they provided a first step toward getting help. That matters. However, as these platforms have grown, important concerns about quality of care, data privacy, and therapist-client matching have emerged — concerns backed by regulatory action and published research.

What the Research and Record Shows

Privacy and Data Concerns

In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) investigated BetterHelp for sharing sensitive mental health data with third-party advertisers including Meta (Facebook), Snapchat, Criteo, and Pinterest — without adequate disclosure to users. BetterHelp agreed to pay $7.8 million in refunds to approximately 800,000 affected customers, with payments beginning in June 2024. Mental health data is among the most sensitive personal information that exists. In a local private practice, your records are held by your clinician and practice — not shared with a technology company's advertising partners.

Therapist Quality and Matching

A 2024 class action lawsuit against BetterHelp alleged that the platform matched patients with therapists who were not licensed to practice in the patient's state and were not qualified to address the patient's stated needs. The suit alleged the platform prioritized growth and availability over appropriate matching. In a local practice, your provider is licensed in your state, known to the community, and accountable to a state licensing board.

Independent surveys and reviews have also found that many users experienced sessions that felt rushed, surface-level, or generic — with therapists frequently late to appointments or slow to respond to messages. Reports also surfaced in 2024 of AI being used in client communications without disclosure.

What the Structure of These Platforms Creates

At large online platforms, therapists are often paid a flat rate per session (as low as $30/hour in reported accounts), are overwhelmed with message-based client communication that goes uncompensated, and face high burnout rates. When therapists burn out, quality of care declines. A private practice with invested, well-compensated clinicians who are embedded in the community creates a fundamentally different therapeutic relationship.

Accountability in Your Community

When you choose a local, licensed practice, your clinician is:

  • Licensed by the Virginia Department of Health Professions (or equivalent state board)
  • Subject to ethics oversight and professional board review
  • Known by name and reputation in the community they serve
  • Able to coordinate directly with your primary care provider, school, or other treating professionals

This level of accountability simply does not exist in the same way with a platform that operates as a tech company first and a therapy provider second.

Online Therapy Is Not Always the Wrong Choice

We are not saying online therapy is never appropriate. For mild anxiety, stress, or general life coaching, a telehealth option — including the telehealth sessions we offer — can be a great fit. The concern is with large, for-profit platforms where accountability, matching quality, data privacy, and therapist working conditions may not meet the standard you deserve.

What to Look For in Any Provider

Whether you choose us or someone else, here is what to verify:

  • Is the provider licensed in Virginia (or your state)? Check the Virginia Department of Health Professions at dhp.virginia.gov
  • What are their specific credentials and specialties?
  • Do they have experience with your particular concern?
  • Are they accountable to a professional licensing board?
  • Is your data protected under HIPAA — not shared with advertisers?
  • Do they offer a consultation before you commit?

Navigating Insurance

Steps to take before your first appointments so you better understand your coverage and reduce unexpected costs.

As a patient or potential patient of Ansari and Associates, LLC, we encourage you — PRIOR to your first or second meeting — to complete the following steps to reduce the likelihood of unexpected out-of-pocket costs. Please note there are NO guarantees that insurance will pay for services even if you follow the steps below, but these are important steps of due diligence.

  1. Call your insurance company and speak with a LIVE person — do not just check online.
  2. Give them your provider's name and ask whether your provider is in-network for behavioral health services.
  3. Make sure your mental health benefits are not covered by a third-party payor. If they are, ask whether your provider is covered by that third-party payor.
  4. Ask whether you have a deductible and how much of it may remain. A deductible is the amount you must pay out-of-pocket before your insurance benefits apply — essentially, it's as if you have no insurance until the deductible is met. A deductible is different from a copay.
  5. Ask what your copay is. A copay is the amount you pay out-of-pocket each visit (or, for psychological testing, when your provider administers tests, scores data, or writes up results — scoring and report writing happen during non-face-to-face time). It's the amount owed once your benefits have kicked in after meeting any deductible. Please bring exact amounts by cash or check; copays are due at the end of each visit.
  6. As an added measure, keep a log of who you spoke with, what they said, and the date, time, and a reference or representative number.
  7. Please let us know immediately if you have any changes to your insurance.

Insurance Terms Explained

Copay

A fixed amount you pay each visit once your insurance coverage has started.

For example, a $30 copay means you pay $30 at each session and your insurance covers the rest.

Deductible

The amount you must pay out-of-pocket before insurance begins covering services.

For example, with a $1,000 deductible, you pay the first $1,000 of care yourself before insurance contributes.

Deductible Reset

Deductibles typically reset at the start of each plan year, so you begin paying toward a new deductible again.

For example, a deductible met in December usually resets that January.

Coinsurance

A percentage of the cost you share with your insurer after your deductible is met.

For example, with 20% coinsurance, you pay 20% of the session cost and insurance pays the remaining 80%.
